*A Complete Course in English Grammar &amp; Composition*

*Docendo Discimus — "by teaching, we learn." After Harvey's Elementary Grammar &amp; Composition (1880).*

### The Course

Grammar is the study of how words are put together to say true and exact things. Over four quarters we work straight through the parts of speech — first meeting each one, then returning to study it in depth — and finish with sentence analysis, syntax, and punctuation. Nineteen lessons, one per week, each drawn from a single classic text and paired with a recorded lecture, an in-app drill, and its own place in the schedule below.

The aim is not to memorize rules but to apply them: to look at any sentence and name what every word is doing in it.

- **Homework is pass / no-credit.** Each of the nineteen lessons has a drill; a flawless 30-question run *aces* the lesson and earns full credit for it. There is no partial credit and no penalty for retrying — ace it whenever you're ready, before that quarter's paper.
- **Papers are the exams.** The two papers in the first half form your **Midterm**; the two in the second half form your **Final**. There is no separate written test.
- **Attendance is watching.** Credit is earned by watching each lecture; short quizzes follow the lectures.

### The Four Papers

Four formal papers — one a quarter — are the writing behind your Midterm and Final. They are arranged to build on one another: first you *tell*, then *explain*, then *argue*, then *judge* — so that by the end writing has become an instrument for your thinking, not merely a set of grammatically correct sentences. Every paper is graded on clear organization, specific detail, and correct grammar, punctuation &amp; spelling — after careful revision.

**Paper I · Narrative — A Significant Experience**
*Quarter 1 · Midterm · 750–1,000 words.* Tell the story of an experience that had a real effect on you. Narrate it in order, with specific detail, and make clear not merely what happened but why it mattered and what you came to understand.

**Paper II · Expository — Explain Something You Know Well**
*Quarter 2 · Midterm · 750–1,000 words.* Explain a subject you know well to a reader who knows nothing about it. Don't persuade — make it understood: define your terms, show how and why it works, and let each paragraph carry one part of the explanation.

**Paper III · Argumentative — The Most Important Purpose of Education**
*Quarter 3 · Final · 750–1,000 words.* Answer one question — what is the most important purpose of education? — and defend the position you actually hold with reasons and evidence. Weigh at least one honest objection and answer it. A strong argument is reasoning, not just a firm opinion.

**Paper IV · Compare · Contrast · Evaluate — Homeschooling vs. Traditional School**
*Quarter 4 · Final · 1,000–1,500 words.* Compare your year of homeschooling with your earlier years in a traditional school. Go past listing differences: explain why they mattered and how they shaped your learning, then judge honestly which is better suited to you, and why.

### The Honor System

The lesson drills are built so that a passing score means you know the material, not the layout. Every attempt draws a fresh set of thirty questions from a larger pool, in random order, with the answer choices shuffled each time — nothing sits in the same place twice. Work honestly; the drills are yours to master, not to game.
